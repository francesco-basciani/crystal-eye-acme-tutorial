# The 2030s: From Event Detection to Event Association

## Observatories

Observatories can record the same astrophysical event through different signals. Electromagnetic telescopes measure radiation across the spectrum, gravitational-wave detectors record changes in spacetime, and neutrino observatories look for neutrinos produced in energetic processes. The figure groups facilities by the kind of signal they observe.

## From detecting to associating events

The slide looks back to 2017 through two examples of multimessenger observations, GW170817 and TXS 0506+056. They motivate the shift from registering gravitational waves, neutrinos or gamma-ray bursts to asking which observations belong to the same event.

The roadmap for the 2030s treats association, localization and characterization as connected tasks. A sky location helps link a gravitational-wave or neutrino event to electromagnetic emission. Prompt emission can then be compared with the later afterglow, and MeV measurements with those at GeV and TeV energies. Repeating these comparisons across many events makes population studies possible. The seminar identifies finding the electromagnetic counterpart as a bottleneck.

## The MeV gap

The chart tracks observational coverage from radio and optical bands through soft and hard X-rays, MeV, GeV and TeV gamma rays. The seminar highlights a gap in sensitive, continuous, wide-field MeV observations. In the seminar’s comparison, Fermi-GBM is described as operating beyond its planned lifetime and having limited sensitivity. The slide raises the possibility of an interruption in MeV coverage in the coming years. It also describes COSI as an imager with a more limited field of view for monitoring. The MeV band matters because it captures prompt GRB emission and the energy range where GRB output can peak, while linking the X-ray and GeV–TeV observations of the transient sky.
